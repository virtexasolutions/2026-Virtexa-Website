An inside sales agent (ISA) is often the first hire a growing real estate team makes. The idea is simple: one person works the phones, reaches new leads fast, finds out who is serious, and sets appointments so the agents can spend their time with clients.

The salary is the number most people look at first. But the real cost of an ISA is the salary plus everything around it: bonuses, payroll costs and benefits, tools, the months it takes to get them productive, and your own time managing them. This guide walks through each piece so you can work out what an ISA would actually cost your business.

## What ISAs are paid

There is no official national figure for real estate ISA pay. The Bureau of Labor Statistics does not track ISAs as their own job category, and published ranges vary a lot depending on the market and whether bonuses are included.

One commonly cited benchmark comes from The Real Estate Trainer, which puts ISAs with no previous experience at roughly **$40,000 to $65,000 a year, salary and bonus included**, and experienced ISAs at **$60,000 to $80,000** on average. ([The Real Estate Trainer](https://therealestatetrainer.com/isa-hiring-inside-sales-agents/))

Treat those as rough estimates. Pay in your market may be higher or lower, and the best ISAs, the ones who consistently set appointments that actually show up and close, tend to cost more.

## How ISA pay is usually structured

Most teams pay ISAs a mix of guaranteed pay and performance pay. The common structures are:

- **Base plus a bonus per appointment.** The ISA earns a salary or hourly rate, plus a bonus for each appointment that is set, or each one that is actually held. Paying on held appointments rewards something the ISA can control while discouraging appointments that never happen.
- **Base plus a share of closed deals.** The ISA earns a bonus when a lead they set closes. This rewards quality, but the payout can come months after the call.
- **Commission only.** Rare, and hard to sustain. Few people stay in a phone role for long without steady income.

Before you settle on a plan, check your state's licensing rules. In many states, an unlicensed assistant cannot be paid a share of a commission, and some conversations an ISA might have, like discussing terms with a seller, may require a license. Your broker can tell you what applies where you are.

## The costs on top of pay

Pay is only part of what an employee costs. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, wages and salaries made up about **70%** of what private employers spent on their workers in June 2026. The other **30%** went to benefits such as paid leave, insurance, retirement contributions, and legally required payroll costs. ([BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.nr0.htm))

That is an average across all private-sector jobs, so your numbers will differ. Still, it is a useful reminder that a $50,000 salary costs your business noticeably more than $50,000.

Then there are the costs that never show up on a payslip:

- **Recruiting.** Job board posts, screening calls, interviews, and test role-plays.
- **Training and ramp-up.** A new ISA needs to learn your market, your scripts, your CRM, and your listings. Expect weeks of lower output before they hit their stride.
- **Tools.** A CRM seat, a phone line or dialer, and any lead-source logins.
- **Management.** Call reviews, coaching, scheduling, and weekly check-ins. This is your time, and it is time you are not spending with clients.
- **Turnover.** Phone roles see plenty of turnover. When an ISA leaves, you pay the recruiting and training costs again, and leads go uncovered while you do.

## A simple worked example

Here is how the numbers can add up for a single full-time ISA. These are illustrative assumptions, not quotes, so swap in your own.

| Cost | Example |
| --- | --- |
| Salary and bonuses | $50,000 |
| Benefits and payroll costs (using the BLS 70/30 average) | about $21,000 |
| Tools: CRM seat, phone line, dialer | $1,500 to $3,000 |
| Recruiting and training (one-time) | a few weeks of your time, plus ad costs |
| Your management time | 2 to 4 hours a week |
| **Total, first year** | **roughly $73,000 or more, plus your time** |

Now divide that by the number of appointments your ISA sets that actually turn into closings. That cost per closing is the figure to compare against your average commission.

## What about an offshore ISA?

Many teams hire virtual assistants overseas to make calls. Rates for real estate cold callers based in the Philippines are commonly listed at around **$6 an hour, or about $960 a month for full-time work**. ([HireTalent.ph](https://hiretalent.ph/tool/boldleads))

That is far cheaper on paper, and for some teams it works well. The trade-offs are worth weighing:

- **Hours.** Unless you pay for a night shift, their working day may not line up with when your leads call.
- **Local knowledge.** Callers ask about neighborhoods, schools, and commutes. That is harder to answer convincingly from abroad.
- **Management.** You still need to train, coach, and review calls, often across time zones.
- **Compliance.** Calling rules such as the TCPA and the Do Not Call registry still apply, and you are responsible for following them.

## How to decide

Ask yourself three questions:

1. **Where do your best leads come from?** If they mostly call in, fast and reliable answering matters most. If you buy online leads that need chasing, outbound follow-up matters more.
2. **When do they come in?** If a large share of calls arrive evenings and weekends, a single ISA on business hours will miss them.
3. **What does a closing cost you today?** Work out your current cost per closed deal, then compare it honestly with each option.
